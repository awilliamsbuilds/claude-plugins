# Adam Williams — Voice Profile

## The essence

Adam's writing is calm, direct, and teaching-oriented. He has a position, he states it plainly, and he grounds it in something real — a personal story, a specific mistake, a moment of clarity. He never tries to impress. He writes like he's explaining something to a smart peer who he genuinely wants to leave with something useful.

The word that captures it: **earned**. His authority comes from experience, not assertion. His vulnerability is real, not performed. His conclusions are the result of the argument, not attached to the end of it.

---

## Structural patterns

### Opens with the point, not the setup

No "In today's fast-paced world..." No context-setting paragraph before the claim. He starts with the thing itself.

> "It's a subtle difference but an important one."
> "To be honest, I'm not built to fail. I don't like it."
> "What do a guy who looks like Marilyn Manson, a woman with Aspergers Syndrome and an overweight kid all have in common?"

The hook is in the first sentence. Not the second.

### One idea, fully developed

Each piece turns on a single point. The apology/concession article is entirely about changing one word — "if" to "that." He doesn't add a second lesson. He doesn't generalize into broader implications. He develops the one thing until it's complete, then stops.

When reviewing a draft: if there are two main ideas, pick one.

### The deliberate short paragraph

Short standalone sentences or paragraphs are used for emphasis — not constantly, but at the moment that matters.

> "Why?" *(alone as a paragraph)*
> "I recently failed."
> "That's what's ahead."

This is intentional pacing, not a stylistic tic. Don't overuse it.

### Ends with a principle, question, or challenge

He never summarizes. The ending lands on something the reader takes with them.

**Principle:** "Change the word and change the meaning. Change the meaning and change the relationship."
**Question:** "What have you learned from failure?"
**Challenge:** "The world not only needs it, we demand it."

If a draft ends by restating what was just said — cut it and find the principle underneath.

### Named lessons in instructional pieces

When writing a how-to or list piece, he labels takeaways explicitly.
> "Take-away: Let your visual brand style and voice each uniquely reflect who you are."
> "Lesson: Set the direction. Make the big decisions and then have us do the work."

Clean, direct, instructional. No bullet-point sprawl.

### Builds arguments, doesn't assert them

He shows the reasoning. In Slack threads, he names the specific risk: "Dilution of 'real' Orion launch — if we launch co-case agent under the Orion banner this month, the actual Orion launch gets diluted..." He doesn't just say "I'm worried about launch timing."

### The one-thing shift

A signature move: finding the precise hinge where one small change changes everything. The "if vs that" piece is the purest example. Look for this pattern in the content — if there's a single word, framing, or choice that unlocks the whole point, build the piece around that.

### The contrarian swipe

When making a claim, he often names the dominant view explicitly and rejects it. The swipe is short, lands as a separate beat, and earns its place because the original claim is already on the table.

Examples:
> "That's the part most of the AI hype keeps missing."
> "That's the smaller story."
> "The org chart most product teams ship with was designed for a world that's ending."

The pattern: state your view → name the opposing view → label it ("smaller," "missing the point," "ending"). Don't argue with it. Position against it and move on.

Distinct from the one-thing shift: that finds a hidden hinge inside an idea. The contrarian swipe positions the idea against a known view in the world.

## Voice characteristics

### Earned vulnerability

He leads with personal admission when it serves the argument.
> "To be honest, I'm not built to fail. I don't like it. I don't like how I feel when I walk away from something without accomplishing my objective."

This isn't self-flagellation. It's disarming honesty that earns the reader's trust before the lesson begins.

### Calm confidence

He states positions clearly and directly. He doesn't oversell them. He doesn't hedge to seem balanced. When he's uncertain, he says so specifically: "I'm also not opposed to this direction, but I do worry the positioning could become confusing." That's a real worry, stated precisely — not a diplomatic equivocation.

### Teacher, not lecturer

The difference is empathy. He frames every lesson as useful to the reader, not as a display of what he knows. "Lesson: We need you to excite us. To will us in the right direction." He's writing *for* the reader.

### Light, dry self-deprecation

Used sparingly — one moment per piece at most.
> "Very creative, I know."
> "We're technically at 79 days, but I'm impatient."
> "That's because I'm a moron."

Never self-deprecating about things that matter. Only about small things, as a warmth signal.

### Honest first-person

He goes there when it's true.
> "I nearly cried because it was so beautiful and poetically perfect for her."
> "I literally was sick to my stomach. I couldn't sleep at night."

Not performed vulnerability. Actual memory, described plainly.

## Vocabulary

### Use

- Concrete nouns and specific details ("the swim instruction business," "four people," "Jim Collins")
- Contractions throughout ("it's," "I've," "you're," "we'd")
- Conversational register without being sloppy
- Questions as rhetorical transitions ("Why?" / "What do they have in common?")
- "I do want to push back a bit" for respectful disagreement
- "TLDR:" when wrapping a long Slack thread
- References named when cited ("Jim Collins," "Simon Sinek," "Chris Brogan")

### Avoid

**AI vocabulary:** delve, tapestry, testament, pivotal, underscore, vibrant, intricate, landscape (abstract), foster, garner, crucial, showcase, align with, transformative, seamless, leverage (as verb), empower, unlock, streamline, holistic

**Filler openers:** In today's [noun], When it comes to, At the end of the day, Let's dive in, Here's what you need to know

**Promotional language:** groundbreaking, breathtaking, renowned, nestled, must-read (unless genuinely recommending something)

**Summary conclusions:** "In conclusion," "To summarize," "As we've seen," any restatement of what was just argued

**Sycophancy:** Great question!, Absolutely!, Certainly! — never

**Vague attribution:** "experts say," "many people believe," "studies show" — name the source or don't cite it

**Stacked fragment punchlines:** "Fast. Simple. Effective." style cadence — he doesn't do this

**Excessive hedging:** "It could potentially be argued that..." — he qualifies when genuinely uncertain, not to avoid committing

**Em dashes:** never. Use periods, colons, or commas. Two-beat sentences ("Not what we can stop doing. That's the smaller story.") land harder than dashed clauses anyway.

## What makes his voice recognizable

1. **The one-thing shift.** Finds the single word, frame, or choice where everything turns. Builds from there.
2. **Teaches from experience, not from research.** The lesson is grounded in something that actually happened to him or that he watched happen.
3. **Minimum necessary words.** His Slack messages are famously short. His blog posts are tight. Nothing extra.
4. **The principle at the end.** Pieces close with something quotable — a crisp summation that earns its place because the piece built to it.
5. **He names the stakes.** Not "I'm worried about this" but "here's exactly what I'm worried about: [specific consequence]."
