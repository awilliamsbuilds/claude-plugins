# Adam Williams — Platform Guide

Platform-specific rules for writing in Adam's voice. Apply after reading voice-profile.md.

---

## LinkedIn posts / Blog posts

### Structure
1. **Opening:** One sentence that starts with the point. Not context. Not a question asking the reader to imagine something. The thing itself.
2. **Body:** One idea, developed through a specific story, example, or observation. Personal experience over research citations where possible. If citing research, name it.
3. **Takeaways (optional):** For instructional pieces, label them. "Take-away:" or "Lesson:" — clean, direct.
4. **Close:** A principle, challenge, or question. Never a summary of what was just said.

### Tone
- Written like a smart person explaining something they actually figured out, to someone they respect
- Opinionated but not preachy — he states a view without moralizing
- The reader should leave with one specific thing they can use or think about

### Length
- Long enough to develop the idea fully, not a word more
- Typically 300–700 words for a standalone post
- Paragraphs vary in length — some are one sentence, some are five

### Do not
- Open with a rhetorical question ("Have you ever wondered...")
- Open announcements with "I'm excited to announce," "Thrilled to share," or "Humbled to..." — open with the announcement itself ("Today I'm announcing...") or the hook behind it
- Name people decoratively. Name them when they did something specific the post is about. A roll-call of gratitude reads as decorative; a list of the people who proved a thesis reads as earned
- Use hashtags as content (they can appear at the end if needed, but not as part of the writing)
- End with "Follow me for more" or explain why to engage
- Use emoji in the body of professional posts

### Example opening (right):
> "It's a subtle difference but an important one."

### Example opening (wrong):
> "In today's business environment, communication has never been more important. As leaders, we're constantly navigating difficult conversations..."

## Slack messages (internal / team-facing)

### General rules
- Short. Fragments are fine. Get to the point in the first line.
- "Hey team!" for team-facing announcements — warm, not performative
- One emoji max, deployed naturally, not as decoration
- TLDR at the end when wrapping a long thread
- Disagree with: "I do want to push back a bit, though" — respectful but substantive
- Ask clarifying questions before redirecting: "Is the goal for this to connect to X, or will these be distinct?"

### For announcements
- State what's happening, why it matters (briefly), what you need from them
- Don't over-explain. Trust them to fill in context.

### For feedback
- Short confirmations can still move things forward: "SG. You still have 4+ years at TRM — your input is welcome."
- Direct when something misses: "Favor, if something is just AI slop, maybe spare me the time of reading it."
- Gives credit specifically: names the person, names the specific behavior, says why it mattered

### For strategic threads
- Names the specific risk or concern, not a general worry
- TLDR at the end if the thread is long: "TLDR: I wouldn't rush to change anything, but..."
- States his position, invites input, doesn't seek consensus

### What not to do
- Don't write Slack messages like emails (no "Hi [name], I wanted to follow up on...")
- Don't over-format with bullets and headers for short thoughts
- Don't pad with pleasantries

## Executive updates / All-hands

### Structure
- Crisp frame first: what are we covering and why it matters — one sentence
- Substance: specifics over generalities; name the initiative, the metric, the team
- Stakes: what it means for the company, stated plainly — not inflated
- What's next: concrete next step, not "we'll continue to monitor"

### Tone
- Calm authority. Not cheerleading, not alarming.
- Honest about complexity: "I'm also not opposed to this direction, but I do worry..."
- Decisive when he has a view. Doesn't death-by-committee the audience.

### Example (right):
> "Cortex is TRM's most ambitious internal technology initiative: a system of AI-driven engines that deliver the outcomes behind our seven strategic pillars."

That's a single sentence that does all the work — names it, scopes it, connects it to strategy. No preamble.

### What not to do
- No "I'm excited to share..." openers
- No corporate filler ("synergies," "alignment," "best practices")
- Don't summarize at the end — close with what's next or what's at stake

## External email

### Tone
- Formal but not stiff. Professional register, human voice.
- Apologizes factually when appropriate: "Apologies for the delay as we've just returned from our sales conference in Sydney." Not "So sorry for any inconvenience this may have caused."
- Delegates clearly and confidently: "I'm looping in [name] from my team, who can take the meeting on my behalf."
- Asks one specific clarifying question rather than a broad open-ended one: "Would you be able to share a bit more detail on the specific limitations you're encountering?"

### Structure
- One paragraph per purpose: context, ask/action, clarifying question
- No lengthy preambles
- Close simply — no "Please don't hesitate to reach out if you have any questions"

### What not to do
- No "I hope this email finds you well"
- No "Please feel free to..."
- No multi-paragraph context-setting before the actual point
