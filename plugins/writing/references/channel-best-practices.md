# Channel Best Practices

Current, voice-neutral best practices for each writing channel the `writing` plugin
composes. The platform skills (`email`, `linkedin`) always load the relevant section
regardless of which personal voice is resolved — so a fresh installer with no voice still
gets current structural guidance. Apply your resolved voice *on top* of these rules; these
govern structure, length, and format, not personality.

None of this is voice-specific. Nothing here assumes a particular person or company.

---

## Email

Personal email — reaching out to people you know, following up, making asks, navigating
sensitive conversations. Not marketing email, newsletters, or cold B2B sequences.

### Subject line
- Be specific about what the email is *about*. "Tuesday's pricing question" beats "Quick
  question," "Following up," "Checking in," or "A thought" — those are content-free and read
  as filler.
- The subject is a promise about the body. Keep it short and concrete.

### Structure
- **Lead with the point or the relationship anchor**, not a pleasantry. The first paragraph
  should carry either the ask or the reason for writing.
- **One paragraph per purpose** — don't fuse context, the ask, and a question into one block.
- **One clear ask per email.** Multiple asks dilute all of them; if you need two things,
  usually you need two emails. Put the ask where it's easy to find, not buried at the bottom.
- Give the recipient exactly what they need to say yes: a specific option, a concrete time, a
  clear next step ("Free Tuesday at 2pm?" not "Let's chat sometime").
- **Close simply** — a plain sign-off or a clear next step. One sign-off, not a stack of three.

### Length and formality
- Match length to the relationship and the purpose. Most personal emails are shorter than the
  drafter thinks. If it's getting long, the ask usually isn't clear yet.
- Match formality to how well you know the recipient. A first-contact email needs a one-line
  anchor ("We met at…") or a clear reason for the outreach; an email to a close contact can
  skip the framing entirely.
- Keep first paragraphs to about three sentences. The longer the opener, the less likely the
  email gets read.

### Avoid
- Throat-clearing openers: "I hope this email finds you well," "Hope you're doing well," "I
  wanted to reach out because…," "Just following up," "I'm writing to…"
- Closing filler: "Please don't hesitate to reach out," "Please feel free to…," "I look
  forward to hearing from you," stacked politeness ("Thanks so much, really appreciate it,
  thanks again").
- Corporate filler: "circle back," "touch base," "per my last email," "at your earliest
  convenience," "going forward."
- Fake personalization — flattery you can't tie to something specific ("I was impressed by
  your recent work"). If you can't name the specific thing, cut it.
- Hedging into mush on a sensitive email. Lead with the relationship, state the point, one
  paragraph per point — don't apologize your way around it.

## LinkedIn — Message

A 1:1 direct message, InMail, or connection note. This is a private, person-to-person
message — **not** a feed post. The single most common failure is writing a message as if it
were a post.

### Rules
- Short and purposeful. State one clear reason for reaching out in the first line or two.
- Lead with why you're writing and what (if anything) you're asking for. Make it easy to
  respond.
- Match register to the relationship — a connection note to a stranger is briefer and more
  context-setting than a message to someone you know.
- A connection-request note works best under ~300 characters and names a genuine, specific
  reason for connecting.

### Do NOT (message-vs-post guard)
- **No feed-post hook mechanics.** No scroll-stopping opener, no curiosity gap, no
  "information-withheld" tease. The recipient already opened the message — there is nothing to
  hook.
- **No engagement bait** ("Thoughts?", "Agree?"), no calls to like/comment/repost — this is a
  private message, not a broadcast.
- **No hashtags.** They do nothing in a DM.
- No one-sentence-per-line post formatting. Write it like a normal message to a person.

## LinkedIn — Post

A native feed post: a public, hook-driven post that develops one idea. This is the default
LinkedIn format and where hook mechanics legitimately belong.

### Post type
Identify which kind of post this is — it shapes the hook and structure:

| Type | What it is |
|------|-----------|
| **Thought leadership** | A position in your field, built around one contrarian or non-obvious claim. |
| **Personal story** | Something that happened. Leads with the moment, not the lesson. |
| **Achievement / milestone** | Announcing something real. Opens with the announcement itself, not "I'm excited to share…" |
| **How-to / instructional** | Step-by-step or lesson-based. Label takeaways explicitly. |
| **Industry take** | A reaction to something happening in the space. Names the thing, takes a position. |

### Hook (the first line)
The first line determines whether the post gets distributed at all — it must stop the scroll
and earn the "…see more" click. Choose a structure that fits the idea; offer a couple of
variants using different formulas:

| Formula | Structure | Example |
|---------|-----------|---------|
| **Opens with the point** | State the claim. Full stop. | "Most teams ship the wrong thing first." |
| **Curiosity gap** | Imply something surprising without giving it away | "I made a call last year I thought would hurt us. It didn't." |
| **Contrarian** | Name the accepted view and reject it in one beat | "Everyone says hire for culture fit. I stopped." |
| **Year-over-year pivot** | Sharp before/after with a time anchor | "A year ago I'd have disagreed with this completely." |
| **The admission** | A specific, honest confession | "I was wrong about this for most of my career." |
| **The one-thing** | Name the single hinge everything turns on | "One word was killing our apologies: 'if.'" |

A hook has to clear two bars, not one: it must win the first-30-minutes distribution *and*
earn dwell time, saves, and real comments afterward. A hook that manufactures curiosity but
delivers nothing specific gets the click and loses the reader in seconds. Make sure there's a
real, referenceable payoff under the hook — a named thing, a number with context, an actual
decision — not just a tease.

### Body
- One idea, developed through a specific story, example, or observation. Firsthand experience
  over generic advice; if you cite research, name it.
- Paragraphs vary in length — some one sentence, some three or four. Real paragraph structure,
  not every-sentence-on-its-own-line.
- A short standalone sentence for emphasis, used at most once.

### Close
- Land on a principle, a question, or a challenge — something new, not a summary of what was
  just said.
- If the post is instructional, label the takeaway ("Take-away:" / "Lesson:").

### Length and format
- Roughly 1,300 characters for short-form, up to ~3,000 for long-form. Go long only when the
  idea genuinely needs it. LinkedIn rewards density.
- Weave one to three hashtags naturally at the end if they help, or drop them — they are not
  content.

### Avoid
- "I'm excited to share," "Thrilled to announce," "Humbled to…" — open with the announcement
  or the hook behind it.
- Rhetorical-question openers ("Have you ever wondered…"), "In today's fast-paced world," and
  similar filler.
- Engagement bait closers ("Agree?", "Thoughts?", "What would you add?", "Repost if this
  resonates," "Follow me for more"). If the post is worth engaging with, people will.
- One-sentence-per-line formatting throughout; arrow chains (→) that read as a slide deck;
  ALL-CAPS single-word emphasis; "Read that again." / "Let that sink in." padding.
- An external link in the post body or "link in comments 👇" — off-platform signals suppress
  reach. If a link matters, add it in the first comment after engagement starts, or describe
  what's there and let people find it.
- The overused achievement formula (emotion word → team thanks → generic universal lesson →
  emoji sign-off). Replace it with a specific story about what made the milestone hard.
- Emoji as decoration in the body of a professional post.
- Summarizing at the end. The close should add something, not restate.

## LinkedIn — Article

A long-form native article published on LinkedIn — closer to a blog post than a feed post.
The reader has already committed to reading; the job is substance and structure, not a
scroll-stopping hook.

### Rules
- **Give it a real title** — descriptive and specific, the way a good essay or blog headline
  is. Not a feed-post hook.
- **Section the body with subheads.** An article is long enough to need navigation; break it
  into a few titled sections that each carry one part of the argument.
- **Open by earning the read through substance**, not hook mechanics. State what the piece is
  about and why it's worth the reader's time — no curiosity-gap tease, no information-withheld
  opener.
- **Write for evergreen shelf life.** Articles are found and read long after publishing, so
  frame the argument to hold up over time rather than reacting to a passing moment.
- Develop the idea fully. Vary paragraph length; use firsthand experience and named sources.
- Close on a principle or a forward-looking point the reader carries out — not a summary.

### Distinct from a feed post
- No scroll-stop hook, no "…see more" mechanics, no one-line-per-paragraph rhythm.
- Longer and more structured: real title, subheads, developed sections.
- No engagement bait or hashtag stacking.
