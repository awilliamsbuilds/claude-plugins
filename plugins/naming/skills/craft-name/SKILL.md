# craft-name: Create Names by How They Sound

## Purpose

Use this skill to create brand, product, app, feature, or project names that feel good to say, are easy to remember, and have enough distinctiveness to stand out.

This skill is especially useful when the name does not need to describe the product literally. The goal is to find names that people enjoy saying, can repeat naturally, and can remember after hearing once or twice.

## Core Principle

A good name is not just a label. It is a spoken experience.

Before judging what a name means, judge how it feels in the mouth:

- Is it easy to say?
- Does it have rhythm?
- Does it feel natural in conversation?
- Does it sound clear when spoken aloud?
- Can someone hear it once and spell it reasonably well?
- Does it feel familiar enough to process but distinct enough to notice?

For this naming style, weight the evaluation roughly:

- **75% mouth feel:** how enjoyable, natural, and effortless the name is to say.
- **25% memorability:** how easily someone can remember it later.

Memorability often follows mouth feel. If a name is satisfying to say, people are more likely to repeat it and remember it.

## Inspiration From Lexicon Branding

Lexicon Branding, the naming company behind names such as Sonos, Swiffer, BlackBerry, Pentium, Dasani, Vercel, and Azure, emphasizes that great names are shaped by both creativity and linguistic science.

Relevant ideas to incorporate:

1. **Sound symbolism matters.**  
   The sounds inside a name can suggest qualities like speed, strength, softness, friendliness, reliability, or innovation before a person knows what the product does.

2. **Processing fluency matters.**  
   A name should be easy for the brain to process. If it is too complex, hard to pronounce, or hard to spell, people may move past it.

3. **Familiar plus surprising is powerful.**  
   Strong names often feel familiar enough to understand but unexpected enough to stand out.

4. **A name does not need to tell the full story.**  
   The name is the foundation for the brand story, not the whole story itself. Supporting copy, visual identity, and product experience can add meaning later.

5. **Avoid the comfort trap.**  
   Names that are too obvious, descriptive, or category-conventional can disappear. A name should not merely fit the category; it should have energy.

6. **Generate broadly before judging.**  
   Lexicon's process emphasizes generating many names across multiple directions before narrowing. Quantity creates the conditions for quality.

7. **Evaluate names in context.**  
   Do not judge names only as a list. Put them into realistic usage: app titles, taglines, onboarding copy, sentences, screenshots, and spoken referrals.

## What Makes a Name Feel Good to Say

### 1. Clear syllable rhythm

Short names usually work best.

Ideal length:

- 1 syllable: bold, crisp, direct.
- 2 syllables: often the sweet spot.
- 3 syllables: acceptable if the rhythm is smooth.

Avoid names over 3 syllables unless there is a strong reason.

Examples of strong rhythm:

- Tempo: **TEM-po**
- Nova: **NO-va**
- River: **RIV-er**
- Sonos: **SO-nos**
- Geneva: **ge-NE-va**

### 2. Consonant-vowel flow

Names with alternating consonant and vowel movement often feel easier to say.

Patterns to look for:

- CV-CV: **Tempo**, **Nova**, **Sano**
- CV-CV-CV: **Geneva**, **Dasani**
- Soft consonant plus open vowel: **Vero**, **Luma**, **Kora**

This structure tends to travel well because it resembles early language patterns and is difficult to mispronounce.

### 3. Open vowel sounds

Open vowels make names feel more breathable and enjoyable.

Useful vowel qualities:

- **A** can feel broad, strong, open.
- **O** can feel round, warm, complete.
- **E** can feel crisp or energetic.
- **I** can feel small, quick, precise.
- **U** can feel deep, heavy, or premium.

Names with strong open vowels often feel more brandable:

- Nova
- Tempo
- Atlas
- Oura
- Sonos

### 4. Avoid awkward consonant clusters

Consonant clusters can make a name feel heavy or difficult to repeat.

This is not always bad. Sometimes heaviness is the point. But if mouth feel is the priority, test clusters carefully.

Potentially heavy clusters:

- **bld** as in Boulder
- **grnd** as in GRND
- **str** as in Strive
- **fr** as in Frame
- **sl** as in Slate

A cluster can work if it creates the right feeling. For example, **Slate** feels crisp and premium. **Boulder** feels strong but heavier to repeat.

### 5. Repeatability

A good name should survive repetition.

Say it 5–10 times quickly:

- Tempo, Tempo, Tempo
- River, River, River
- Motion, Motion, Motion
- Boulder, Boulder, Boulder

Notice whether it stays pleasant or starts to feel awkward, blurry, or tiring.

A name that remains enjoyable after repetition has strong brand potential.

### 6. Sentence fit

Test the name in natural spoken sentences.

Use phrases like:

- "I use ___."
- "Track it in ___."
- "Open ___."
- "Send it through ___."
- "Check ___."
- "I found it on ___."
- "Download ___."

The name should feel natural in everyday conversation.

For an app, this test is especially important because users may recommend it verbally.

### 7. Hearability and spellability

A name can look great but fail when spoken aloud.

Ask:

- If someone hears it once, can they spell it?
- Will autocorrect fight it?
- Does it sound like a more famous brand?
- Does it trigger unwanted search results?
- Does it require explanation every time?

Examples of possible friction:

- **Vitus** may be heard as Vytus.
- **GRND** may be associated with Grindr or may be hard to pronounce.
- **Forma** may be crowded or generic in fitness contexts.

Spelling does not have to be perfect, but friction should be intentional and worth it.

### 8. Compound-word seam test

Run this test whenever a candidate joins two whole, recognizable words (e.g., **Headroom**, **Snapchat**, **Codebase**, **Facebook**). This is a distinct failure mode from clusters inside a single word (see above) — it's about the boundary between two words, and it's easy to miss because the name reads fine and means something coherent on the page.

At the seam, where word A ends and word B begins, ask:

- Do the ending sound of word A and the starting sound of word B collide? Watch especially for a stop consonant (**d, t, b, p, g, k**) running into a liquid or approximant (**r, l, w, y**) — this is the exact failure in **head-room**, where the **d** has nowhere clean to go before **r**.
- Does word A's final consonant get weakened, swallowed, or dropped in natural fast speech?
- Say the full name 5–10 times quickly. Does the seam stay crisp, or does it start to blur ("heh-room," "hedroom")?
- Does the name land as one fused sound unit, or does the listener's ear keep parsing it back into "word A" + "word B"? Names like **Tempo**, **Sonos**, and **Canopy** arrive as a single shape; a name with a bad seam behaves like two words standing next to each other.
- Cover the components mentally and just say the whole word: does it feel like a natural single word, or like a description read aloud?

Do this test *especially* when the compound's meaning is a perfect conceptual fit for the product — semantic fit is exactly what makes evaluators overlook a bad seam. A name can be intellectually precise and phonetically unsatisfying at the same time; meaning does not get to compensate for a collision at the seam. If the seam collides, either swap in a word with a smoother transition, drop the compound structure for an invented or root-derived name, or flag the trade-off explicitly to the user rather than recommending the name outright.

## Naming Directions to Explore

Do not begin by searching only inside the product category. Great names often come from adjacent or unrelated territories.

Explore these directions:

### 1. Real everyday words

These can be powerful because they are easy to say and remember.

Examples:

- Tempo
- River
- Slate
- Frame
- Motion
- Boulder
- Orange
- Apple
- Stripe

Use real words when you want maximum processing fluency.

### 2. Metaphorical product-adjacent words

These do not describe the product literally but connect emotionally.

For a workout tracker:

- Tempo: rhythm, consistency, pace.
- Forge: transformation, strength, craft.
- Ember: fire, energy, beginnings.
- Atlas: strength, endurance, carrying weight.
- Boulder: mass, strength, groundedness.

### 3. Root-derived invented words

Start with a meaningful root, then change 1–2 letters to make the name more ownable.

Examples:

- Sonos from sound / sonus.
- Dasani using "san" as a health-related root.
- Vitus from vita / life.
- Valen from valens / strong or healthy.

Use this direction when real words are too crowded but you still want the name to feel grounded.

### 4. Unrelated but phonetic names

Sometimes the best name has no obvious connection to the product. It simply feels good to say.

Qualities to prioritize:

- Short.
- Smooth.
- Clear vowels.
- Easy rhythm.
- Low spelling friction.
- Distinctive search profile.

### 5. Cross-category inspiration

Borrow from unrelated worlds:

- Nature
- Music
- Architecture
- Geography
- Materials
- Tools
- Mythology
- Food
- Sports
- Astronomy
- Weather
- Textiles
- Navigation

This helps avoid category clichés.

## Generation Process

### Step 1: Define the target feeling

Do not start with words. Start with the desired sensation.

Choose 3–5 adjectives:

- Clean
- Premium
- Strong
- Calm
- Fast
- Warm
- Precise
- Playful
- Durable
- Minimal
- Energetic
- Trustworthy

Example for Tempo:

- Simple
- Rhythmic
- Consistent
- Progress-oriented
- Easy to say

### Step 2: Define what to avoid

Clarify what the name should not feel like.

Examples:

- Not bro-y.
- Not clinical.
- Not overly descriptive.
- Not hard to spell.
- Not crowded in the App Store.
- Not too similar to dating apps or social apps.
- Not locked into one feature if the product may expand.

### Step 3: Generate across territories

Create lists in separate buckets. Do not judge too early.

Suggested buckets:

1. Real words.
2. Short nature words.
3. Musical/rhythm words.
4. Materials and objects.
5. Latin/Greek roots.
6. Invented root-based names.
7. Completely unrelated phonetic names.
8. Strong one-syllable words.
9. Smooth two-syllable words.
10. Three-syllable words with clear rhythm.

Generate at least 100 names before narrowing.

### Step 4: Run the mouth-feel screen

For each candidate, score 1–5:

| Criterion | Question | Score |
|---|---|---|
| Ease | Is it effortless to say? | 1–5 |
| Rhythm | Does it have a pleasing beat? | 1–5 |
| Repeatability | Does it stay pleasant after 5–10 repetitions? | 1–5 |
| Sentence fit | Does it sound natural in real usage? | 1–5 |
| Hearability | Can people understand it when spoken? | 1–5 |
| Spellability | Can people spell it after hearing it? | 1–5 |
| Distinctiveness | Does it stand out without feeling weird? | 1–5 |
| Seam clarity (compounds only) | If it's two joined words, does the seam stay crisp under repetition, or collide/blur? | 1–5 |

Give extra weight to ease, rhythm, repeatability, and sentence fit. For any candidate that joins two whole words, a low seam-clarity score is disqualifying on its own — a bad seam should not be averaged away by strong scores elsewhere, since a good concept with a colliding seam is exactly how a name like "Headroom" clears every other filter and still fails in the mouth.

### Step 5: Run the natural-language test

Say each finalist aloud in sentences.

For an app:

- "I use ___."
- "Open ___."
- "I track my workouts in ___."
- "Download ___."
- "Check your progress in ___."
- "I switched to ___."

For a company:

- "I work at ___."
- "We built it with ___."
- "___ raised a seed round."
- "___ is launching today."

For a product:

- "Try ___."
- "I bought ___."
- "___ works better."
- "The new ___ is out."

If the name feels awkward inside real sentences, downgrade it.

### Step 6: Test repetition

Repeat the name quickly 10 times.

Watch for:

- Tongue fatigue.
- Blurring.
- Awkward consonants.
- Confusion with other words.
- Loss of charm.
- For compound names: does the seam between the two words hold up, or does it start to slur ("head-room" → "hedroom")? Run the compound-word seam test explicitly if it applies.

Names that still feel good after repetition are stronger.

### Step 7: Test visual shape

Write the name in multiple cases:

- Tempo
- TEMPO
- tempo
- Tempo: Workout Tracker

Check:

- Does it look balanced?
- Does it look premium?
- Does it create awkward letter shapes?
- Does it work in an app icon?
- Does it look good next to a descriptive subtitle?

Spelling can change perception even when pronunciation stays the same. A name should sound good and look good.

### Step 8: Put the name in context

Do not choose from a bare list.

Create simple proof-of-concept lines:

- App Store title
- Tagline
- Website hero
- App onboarding line
- Notification copy
- Social bio

Example:

- **Tempo: Workout Tracker**
- **Find Your Rhythm.**
- **Built for Progress.**
- **Progress Has a Rhythm.**
- **Find Your Tempo. Track workouts, build consistency, and see your progress over time.**

Context reveals whether the name has room to become a brand.

### Step 9: Check marketplace friction

Before falling in love, check:

- App Store search.
- Google Play search.
- Domain availability.
- Social handles.
- Obvious search engine conflicts.
- Trademark databases.
- Similar names in adjacent categories.
- Similar names with negative associations.

Do not rely only on exact matches. Look for similarity in:

- Sound.
- Spelling.
- Meaning.
- Category.
- Visual impression.

### Step 10: Choose the name with the best total experience

The best name may not be the most descriptive name.

Choose the name that:

- Feels best to say.
- Is easy to process.
- Has enough distinctiveness.
- Creates room for a story.
- Works in real product contexts.
- Avoids obvious legal and marketplace friction.

## Quick Phonetic Heuristics

Use these as loose guides, not hard rules.

### Softer / friendlier sounds

- M
- N
- L
- R
- S
- V
- W

These can feel smooth, warm, premium, or approachable.

### Stronger / punchier sounds

- B
- D
- G
- K
- P
- T

These can feel more physical, active, durable, or forceful.

### Futuristic / energetic letters

- X
- Z
- V
- K

Use carefully. They can add edge, but too much can feel artificial or dated.

### Smooth endings

- -a
- -o
- -er
- -us
- -um

Smooth endings can make a name easier to say repeatedly.

### Crisp endings

- -t
- -k
- -x
- -p
- -d

Crisp endings add punch but may reduce softness.

## Warning Signs

Be careful when a name:

- Requires pronunciation instructions.
- Looks better than it sounds.
- Sounds better than it spells.
- Is hard to say repeatedly.
- Blurs when spoken quickly.
- Is too close to a famous app or brand.
- Has unwanted search associations.
- Is overly descriptive and category-generic.
- Feels clever once but annoying later.
- Depends on a joke or trend.
- Locks the product into a narrow feature.
- Joins two real words at a seam where a stop consonant (d, t, b, p, g, k) runs into a liquid or approximant (r, l, w, y) — say it 5x fast to check ("Headroom" is the reference case: the "d" into "r" transition slurs).
- Has a strong conceptual/semantic fit that could be masking a phonetic problem — meaning should never substitute for the seam and repetition tests.

## Example: Tempo

### Why it works

**Tempo** is strong because:

- It has two syllables.
- It has a clean consonant-vowel rhythm.
- It is easy to say and spell.
- It feels natural in conversation.
- It has a built-in metaphor: rhythm, pace, consistency.
- It connects to workouts without being generic fitness language.
- It can support broader brand messaging.

### Usage hierarchy

**App Store title:**  
Tempo: Workout Tracker

**Primary tagline:**  
Find Your Rhythm.

**Product promise:**  
Built for Progress.

**Brand philosophy:**  
Progress Has a Rhythm.

**Campaign / onboarding line:**  
Find Your Tempo.  
Track workouts, build consistency, and see your progress over time.

### Why this hierarchy works

- **Tempo: Workout Tracker** is clear and functional.
- **Find Your Rhythm** reinforces the name without repeating it.
- **Built for Progress** explains the product benefit.
- **Progress Has a Rhythm** gives the brand a point of view.
- **Find Your Tempo** works best when not placed directly under the word Tempo, because otherwise it repeats the name too closely.

## Final Decision Rule

When choosing between finalists, do not ask only, "What does this mean?"

Ask:

> Would someone enjoy saying this name enough to repeat it?

If the answer is yes, the name has a real chance to become memorable.

Do not let a strong conceptual fit push a finalist to the top of the recommendation before it has passed the repetition test and, if it's a compound, the seam test out loud. A name that is conceptually perfect and phonetically weak (see the compound-word seam test) should be presented with that trade-off named explicitly, not recommended as the clear winner.
